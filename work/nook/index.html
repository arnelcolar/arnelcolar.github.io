UX Case Study · Mobile App

NOOK: a furniture app that gets shoppers from browsing to checkout in fewer steps.

A mobile shopping concept for a furniture retailer, with a small catalog, real product photos, and a checkout meant to take under a minute. This page explains the design choices and links to a clickable prototype.

PlatformiOS / Android · 390×844

The problem

01 / 10

Furniture costs a lot and is hard to judge on a phone, yet many furniture apps look like desktop catalogs squeezed onto a small screen. The grids are dense, the dimensions are unclear, you have to make an account before checkout, and you can't tell how a piece will look in your room.

  • Shoppers abandon carts when shipping and tax only show up at the last step.
  • Having to create an account is one of the most common reasons shoppers give for leaving checkout.
  • Huge, unsorted catalogs make choosing harder when people already worry about price, fit, and style.
  • Generic stock photos make a listing harder to trust when material and finish matter.

NOOK keeps the catalog small, uses honest photos, shows the full price at every step, and asks only for what the purchase needs.

Mobile details

06 / 10
Add to Cart, Checkout, and the tab bar sit in the bottom third of the screen, where a thumb reaches them without shifting grip.
  • Every tappable control (nav icons, quantity stepper, swatches) is at least 44×44px, the platform minimum, even when the icon looks smaller.
  • The Checkout and Add to Cart buttons stay in a sticky footer, so nobody scrolls to reach them.
  • The tab bar has five destinations (Home, Search, Saved, Cart, Profile), the most it can hold before it needs a More menu.
  • Dark mode swaps the design tokens instead of using a separate design. See the design system section.
  • When the connection drops, the app shows a no-connection screen instead of a blank or broken one (see edge cases).
  • Ink-on-white and white-on-gold pairs pass WCAG AA contrast. Each tab bar icon has one clear meaning and does not rely on color alone.

Edge cases

07 / 10

Design pitches often skip these states: an empty cart, a slow connection, a search with no matches. Each one has its own screen here.

Loading. A skeleton holds the layout while the detail screen loads, so there is no blank white flash.
Empty search. A clear "no results" message with a next step, instead of a blank grid.
Empty cart. A short message says the cart is empty, instead of showing a blank screen.
No connection. Explains what happened, tells the shopper their cart is safe, and offers a Retry button.
Dark mode. The tokens switch to dark values, so it needed no second design.

Expected impact

09 / 10

NOOK is a new concept, so there are no before-and-after numbers. Each choice below follows a common ecommerce checkout pattern.

Guest checkout
Shoppers can buy without making an account, which removes a common reason for abandoned carts.
Costs shown early
Tax and shipping appear in the cart, before checkout, so the final total holds no surprises.
Search-first path
A shopper who already knows what they want reaches checkout in 4 taps, without browsing the full catalog.

Once the app is live, the plan is to measure each of these with real checkout data (see next steps).

Next steps

10 / 10
  • Moderated usability tests with 5 to 8 target shoppers on this prototype.
  • Real catalog items, copy, and returns and shipping terms in place of the placeholders.
  • A payment provider (Stripe or Shop Pay) for the live checkout step.
  • Component specs and design tokens from section 08, ready for a developer.
  • An accessibility audit: screen-reader labels and a final contrast check before the build.
  • Checkout tracking, to test the expected impact once the app is live.
